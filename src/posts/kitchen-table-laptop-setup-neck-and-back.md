For a long stretch, my "office" was the end of the dining table. Laptop flat on the timber, kitchen chair, fruit bowl pushed to one side. By Thursday afternoons my neck felt like it had been folded in half.

Plenty of us still work this way, at least some of the time — in a rental with no study, a share house, or a unit where the second bedroom is already a bedroom. You don't need a dedicated room or a new desk to make it better. You need to fix a handful of specific problems, roughly in this order.

I'm not a physio or an ergonomist. This is general setup advice drawn from workplace health and safety guidance and a lot of trial and error. If you already have pain that isn't going away, see a GP or physio.

## Problem 1: you're looking down all day

This is the big one. A laptop on a table puts the screen well below eye level, so your head tips forward for hours. Your keyboard and screen are joined together, which means you can't get both in a good spot at once.

**The fix:** raise the screen and separate the keyboard.

- Put the laptop up on a stand, or on a stack of sturdy books, so the **top of the screen sits at roughly eye level or slightly below**.
- Add a **separate keyboard and mouse** on the table in front of you. They don't need to be fancy. A basic wired set works fine.
- Sit about an arm's length from the screen. If you're leaning in to read, increase the text size instead of moving closer.

This one change made more difference to my neck than everything else combined. If you only do one thing from this post, do this.

If you wear multifocal glasses, you may find you tilt your head back to read a raised screen. Try the screen a little lower, and talk to your optometrist about whether a dedicated pair for computer distance would suit you.

## Problem 2: the chair is too low (or too hard, or both)

Dining chairs are designed for a meal, not an eight-hour day. Most tables are a fixed height, so the chair has to do the adjusting.

**The fix:** adjust what you sit on, then support your feet.

- Aim for your **elbows to be around the height of the keyboard**, with your shoulders relaxed rather than hunched up.
- If you're too low, a firm cushion raises you. A folded towel works in a pinch.
- Once you've raised the seat, check your feet. They should rest flat. If they're dangling, use a footrest — a sturdy box, a step or a stack of old magazines all do the job.
- For your lower back, a small cushion or rolled-up towel behind the curve of your back can make a hard chair much more bearable.

If you've got the budget for one proper purchase, an adjustable office chair is usually a better spend than a new desk, because you can set it to the table you already have.

## Problem 3: glare and dodgy light

Kitchen and dining areas often have big windows or sliding doors, and in a Queensland summer the morning light is intense. A bright window behind your screen makes you squint; one directly behind *you* bounces off the screen.

**The fix:** sit side-on to the window.

- Turn your setup so the window is to your left or right, not in front of or behind you.
- Use a blind, sheer curtain or the table's position to soften direct sun.
- In the evening, add a lamp so the room isn't dark around a bright screen.

Side-on to a window also helps with video calls. Your face is lit evenly, rather than you appearing as a silhouette.

## Problem 4: cables everywhere and nowhere to plug in

Dining tables are rarely near a power point. The result: a charger cable stretched across a walkway, waiting for someone carrying a hot cup of tea.

**The fix:** route power out of the traffic path.

- Use a power board with a decent cord length placed under the table, and run the cord along the wall or table leg rather than across the floor.
- Avoid daisy-chaining one power board into another. Electrical safety bodies across Australia advise against it because of the overload and fire risk.
- A couple of reusable cable ties or a cable clip on the table leg keep things tidy.

## Problem 5: you never actually stop

At an office desk, there's a walk to the kitchen, a meeting room, a chat. At the dining table, the kitchen is two steps away and the day can slide past without you moving.

**The fix:** build in movement on purpose.

- Change position often. Workplace health guidance generally recommends breaking up long periods of sitting rather than staying in one posture all day.
- Take calls standing up, or walking around the yard if you don't need the screen.
- Set a gentle reminder, or tie movement to something you already do: every time you refill your water, take a lap of the house.
- In summer, keep that water bottle on the table. Warm rooms and air-con both dry you out, and thirst sneaks up on you while you're concentrating.

## Problem 6: the table is also the dinner table

The overlooked part of working from the dining table is that it has to stop being an office at some point. If packing down is a hassle, the laptop stays out, work bleeds into the evening, and dinner happens around a mouse and a pile of notes.

**The fix:** make pack-down a two-minute job.

- Keep everything in one **box or tub**: stand, keyboard, mouse, charger, notebook, pens. When work ends, it all goes in, lid on, onto a shelf.
- Leave the power board under the table so there's nothing to unplug except the laptop.
- If you use a cushion or footrest, give it a home nearby.

Having a ritual for the end of the day helps more than you'd expect. Closing the lid on the box is a clear signal that work is done.

## A short shopping list (only if you need it)

You may already have most of this at home. If not, these are the items that make the biggest difference, in rough order of value:

1. Laptop stand (or sturdy books)
2. Separate keyboard and mouse
3. Firm seat cushion and a footrest
4. Small lumbar cushion
5. Power board with a longer cord
6. A storage tub for pack-down
7. A desk lamp

None of it has to match. My stand was a stack of two cookbooks and an old hardcover dictionary for the best part of a year, and it worked.

## The quick check

Sit down at your table setup and run through this:

- Is the top of the screen at or just below eye level?
- Are your elbows near keyboard height, shoulders relaxed?
- Are your feet flat on the floor or a footrest?
- Is the window beside you rather than in front or behind?
- Are cables out of the walkway?
- Could you pack it all away in two minutes?

If you can tick most of those, your kitchen table is doing a decent impression of a real desk.
