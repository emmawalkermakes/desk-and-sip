I've killed more desk plants than I'd like to admit. A fern that went crispy in a week. A basil plant that never stood a chance. A succulent I drowned out of pure enthusiasm. Offices are strange places for plants: the air-conditioning runs all day, the light is often fluorescent or LED, and in summer the building might be closed for a week or two over Christmas.

After a few years of trial and error, I've settled on a small group of plants that cope, and a care routine that takes about two minutes a week. If you're about to brighten up your desk before the end-of-year rush, here's what I've learned.

## Why offices are hard on plants

Three things work against you.

**Dry air.** Air-conditioning tends to dry the air, which is why your skin and eyes feel it after a long day. Plants that love humidity, like many ferns and calatheas, often get brown, crispy leaf edges in this environment.

**Low or artificial light.** Unless you sit right by a window, your desk may get far less light than it seems. Our eyes adjust to dim rooms; plants don't.

**Irregular care.** Weekends, leave, working from home a few days a week, and the Christmas shutdown all mean your plant goes without attention for stretches.

So the goal is a plant that tolerates dry air, lower light and a bit of neglect.

## Forgiving plants for a desk

These are widely recommended as low-maintenance indoor plants and are easy to find at Australian nurseries and hardware stores.

### ZZ plant (Zamioculcas zamiifolia)
Glossy leaves, upright shape, and a reputation as one of the toughest indoor plants around. It stores water in thick roots, so it copes well with being forgotten. It's my top pick for a desk away from the window.

### Snake plant (Sansevieria, now often listed as Dracaena)
Tall, architectural and very tolerant of low light and dry air. Like the ZZ, it prefers being underwatered to overwatered. Smaller varieties suit a desk corner.

### Devil's ivy (Epipremnum aureum, often sold as pothos)
A trailing plant that looks great spilling off a shelf or monitor riser. It tells you when it's thirsty by drooping a little, then perks up after a drink, which makes it ideal for beginners.

### Peace lily (Spathiphyllum)
Handles lower light and will flower indoors. It's a bit thirstier than the others and droops dramatically when dry, so it suits someone who's at their desk most days.

### Small cacti and succulents
Only if you have a bright spot near a window. In a dim office they stretch and lose their shape. They're the plant most often killed by overwatering.

**A note on pets and kids:** several common indoor plants, including peace lilies, devil's ivy, ZZ plants and snake plants, are generally listed as toxic to cats and dogs if chewed. If you're choosing a plant for a home desk with pets around, check a reliable source before you buy.

## A two-minute weekly routine

This is what has kept my current desk plants alive for over a year.

1. **Pick one day.** I check mine on Monday morning, before the inbox wins.
2. **Finger test.** Push a finger into the soil up to the second knuckle. If it's dry, water. If it's still damp, leave it. This one habit prevents most deaths, because overwatering kills more office plants than neglect.
3. **Water at the sink.** Water slowly until it drains from the bottom, let it drip for a minute, then put it back. A pot with a drainage hole, sitting in a saucer, makes this far easier.
4. **Wipe the leaves.** Office dust settles on leaves and blocks light. A damp cloth once a fortnight helps.
5. **Turn the pot.** A quarter turn each week stops it leaning towards the light.

## Getting through the Christmas shutdown

This is the big one. Many Australian workplaces close or run on a skeleton crew between Christmas and New Year, which often coincides with the hottest weeks of the year.

A few options, from simplest to most involved:

- **Take it home.** If it's small, this is the safest choice.
- **Water well before you leave.** For tough plants like ZZ and snake plants, a thorough watering the day before the break is often enough for a couple of weeks.
- **Move it away from the window.** Direct summer sun through glass, with the air-con off, can cook a plant.
- **Use a self-watering pot or watering spikes.** These release water slowly and buy you extra time for thirstier plants.
- **Ask a colleague who's working.** A quick message and a sticky note with simple instructions ("water if the soil is dry") goes a long way.

Check whether your building turns off the air-conditioning over the break. A closed office can get very warm, and that changes how fast plants dry out.

## Making it look good

A plant earns its place on your desk when it looks like part of the setup rather than an afterthought.

- **Match the pot to your desk.** A plain ceramic pot in a colour that echoes your mug or desk mat ties things together.
- **Give it a riser.** Putting a small plant on a stack of books or a little stand lifts it into your eye line.
- **Keep it away from the keyboard.** Water and electronics don't mix. Leave a gap between the saucer and anything with a cable.
- **Choose a size that fits.** A plant that blocks your second monitor will be moved to the floor within a week.

## A good place to start

If you're new to desk plants, start with one devil's ivy or ZZ plant in a pot with a drainage hole, do the finger test every Monday, and see how you go. Once you've kept it alive through a summer, you've earned the right to try something more demanding.
