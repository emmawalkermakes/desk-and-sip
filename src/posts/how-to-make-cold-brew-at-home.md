When the afternoon heat hits and a hot coffee is the last thing you want, cold brew is a good answer. It isn't just hot coffee poured over ice. It's coffee steeped in cold water for many hours, which gives a smoother, less sharp cup that a lot of people find easier to drink over ice.

The best part for desk days: you make a big batch once, keep it in the fridge, and your iced coffee is ready in about thirty seconds. No café run in the heat.

Here's the whole process, step by step, using gear most kitchens already have.

## What you need

- **Coffee**: whole beans you grind yourself, or pre-ground coffee labelled for plunger (French press), which is coarse.
- **Cold water**: filtered if your tap water has a strong taste.
- **A large jar or jug with a lid**: a big glass jar, a jug or a plunger.
- **Something to strain with**: a fine sieve plus a paper coffee filter, a clean cheesecloth, or the plunger itself.
- **A clean bottle or jar** to store the finished brew.
- **Kitchen scales** (helpful, but not essential).

## Step 1: Get the grind right

Use a **coarse** grind, roughly like raw sugar or coarse breadcrumbs. Fine espresso grounds turn cold brew muddy and bitter, and they're a nightmare to strain.

If you're buying ground coffee, ask your local roaster or café to grind it for plunger or cold brew. Most are happy to.

## Step 2: Choose a starting ratio

Recipes vary a lot, and there's no single correct number. Two common approaches:

- **Concentrate**: a strong brew of roughly **1 part coffee to 5 parts water by weight** (for example, 100 g coffee to 500 g water), which you dilute when serving. It takes up less fridge space and gives you more control.
- **Ready to drink**: closer to **1 part coffee to 8 parts water**, which you can pour straight over ice.

No scales? Measure by volume and keep notes. The aim is a repeatable starting point that you adjust to taste, not a perfect first batch.

## Step 3: Combine and stir

Put the grounds in your jar, pour in the cold water, and stir gently so that every bit of coffee gets wet. Dry clumps floating on top won't brew properly. Give it another stir after a few minutes if needed.

Put the lid on.

## Step 4: Steep in the fridge

Steep it **in the fridge for around 12 to 24 hours**. Many guides suggest steeping at room temperature, but in a Queensland summer, "room temperature" can get very warm. The fridge keeps things safer and more predictable.

As a rough guide:

- **Shorter steep (about 12 hours)**: lighter and brighter.
- **Longer steep (closer to 24 hours)**: stronger and heavier. Go too far past that and it can start to taste woody or bitter.

Starting it after dinner and straining it the next evening is an easy rhythm.

## Step 5: Strain, twice if needed

This is the fiddly part, but it makes the biggest difference to how the coffee tastes.

1. **First pass**: pour through a fine sieve (or press the plunger) to catch the bulk of the grounds.
2. **Second pass**: pour again through a paper coffee filter or cheesecloth to remove the fine sediment.

Paper filters are slow with a big batch. Let gravity do the work and don't squeeze the grounds, because squeezing pushes bitterness and silt into the brew. Used grounds can go in the compost.

## Step 6: Store it properly

Pour the strained cold brew into a clean, sealed bottle or jar and keep it in the fridge. Label it with the date. Most guides suggest using it within about a week to two weeks, and it tends to taste best in the first few days. If it smells or tastes off, tip it out.

## Step 7: Serve it your way

If you made a concentrate, start by mixing **about 1 part concentrate with 1 part water or milk** over ice, then adjust. Ideas to try:

- **Straight over ice** with a splash of milk.
- **Topped with cold milk** or your favourite plant milk.
- **Cold brew ice cubes**: freeze some of the brew in an ice cube tray so your drink doesn't get watery as the ice melts.
- **A little sweetness**: sugar doesn't dissolve well in cold drinks, so use a simple syrup (equal parts sugar and hot water, stirred until dissolved, then cooled).

Remember that cold brew concentrate is strong. Dilute it properly and keep an eye on how many glasses you've had, especially later in the day.

## Fixing a batch that isn't right

| Problem | Likely cause | Try next time |
|---|---|---|
| Weak or watery | Too little coffee, or too short a steep | More coffee, or a longer steep |
| Bitter or harsh | Grind too fine, steeped too long, or grounds squeezed | Coarser grind, shorter steep, gentle strain |
| Muddy or gritty | Fines getting through | Add a paper-filter second pass |
| Flat or dull | Stale beans | Fresher beans, ideally used within a few weeks of the roast date |
| Sour or thin | Under-extracted, usually from too short a steep | Steep a few hours longer |

Change one thing at a time so you know which tweak helped.

## Desk-friendly tips

- **Use an insulated tumbler or bottle** so your coffee stays cold through a long meeting.
- **Pop a coaster under it.** Iced drinks sweat, and keyboards don't like drips.
- **Bring a small bottle of concentrate** if you work in an office with a fridge. Add water, milk and ice there.
- **Make it a Sunday habit.** One batch on the weekend can cover the working week.

Once you've done it a couple of times, the whole process takes about ten minutes of actual work, spread over two days. You'll have something cold and good waiting in the fridge when the afternoon heat hits.
