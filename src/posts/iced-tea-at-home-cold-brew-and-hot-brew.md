A jug of iced tea in the fridge is one of the cheapest, easiest upgrades to a hot workday. It's less sweet than most bottled versions, you control the strength, and it takes a couple of minutes of actual effort.

There are two main ways to make it. Both work. They just suit different teas, different moods and different amounts of planning ahead.

## The quick comparison

| | Cold-brew | Hot-brew, then chill |
|---|---|---|
| **Effort** | Almost none | A few minutes with the kettle |
| **Time until ready** | Several hours (overnight is easiest) | About an hour or so to chill, or straight away over plenty of ice |
| **Flavour** | Smooth, mellow, rarely bitter | Stronger and brighter, more "tea-like" |
| **Best for** | Green tea, white tea, oolong, delicate herbal blends | Black tea, robust blends, spiced or fruity teas |
| **Common problem** | Weak if steeped too short | Bitter if steeped too long or too hot |

## Method 1: Cold-brew iced tea

This is the lazy option, and it's very forgiving.

1. **Add tea to a jug or large jar.** As a starting point, use a little more tea than you would for the same amount of hot tea, because cold water extracts flavour more slowly. Loose-leaf or tea bags both work.
2. **Fill with cold water,** filtered if your tap water tastes strongly of chlorine.
3. **Cover and refrigerate** for roughly 6 to 12 hours. Greens and whites tend to need less time; black teas and herbals can go longer.
4. **Strain or remove the bags.** Don't leave the tea in indefinitely.
5. **Taste and adjust.** Too strong? Add water. Too weak? Use more tea next time, or steep longer.

Start it after dinner and it's ready when you sit down at your desk the next morning.

**Why it's smoother:** cold water pulls out less of the compounds that make tea taste harsh, so even a long cold steep is rarely as bitter as an over-steeped hot cup.

## Method 2: Hot-brew, then chill

This one is closer to normal tea-making, and gives a bolder result.

1. **Brew a strong tea concentrate.** Use about double your usual amount of tea for a smaller volume of water. You'll be diluting it with ice or cold water later.
2. **Mind the water temperature.** Black tea and most herbal teas are fine with just-boiled water. Green and white teas are better with water that's cooled a bit (many packets suggest somewhere around 70 to 80°C), because boiling water can make them bitter.
3. **Time it.** Follow the packet as a guide. Over-steeping is the main cause of bitter iced tea, so set a timer.
4. **Remove the tea** promptly.
5. **Dilute and cool.** Pour the concentrate into a jug of cold water, or over a glass packed with ice. Once it's cooled a little, move the jug to the fridge.

If you want a glass right now, this is the method: brew strong, pour over lots of ice, done.

## What about "sun tea"?

You might see recipes that leave a jar of tea in the sun to brew. Food safety advice generally recommends against it, because warm water sitting out for hours is a good environment for bacteria to grow. In an Australian summer, the fridge method is the safer choice.

## Troubleshooting

**It's bitter.** Steeped too long, water too hot (for green or white tea), or too much tea. Shorten the steep first, or switch to cold-brew.

**It's cloudy.** Hot-brewed tea, especially black tea, can turn cloudy when it's chilled. It's caused by natural compounds in the tea and is harmless, but if you prefer it clear, try cold-brewing, or let hot tea cool to room temperature before it goes in the fridge rather than chilling it suddenly. A splash of hot water can sometimes clear it.

**It's weak.** Use more tea rather than steeping for much longer, which can bring bitterness along with strength.

**It's watery by the afternoon.** Make tea ice cubes: freeze some of the tea in an ice-cube tray so it doesn't dilute as it melts.

**It tastes stale.** Iced tea doesn't keep for long. Keep it covered in the fridge, drink it within a few days, and tip it out if it smells or tastes off.

## Flavour ideas without much effort

- **Lemon or lime slices** added to the glass, not the jug, so the pith doesn't make the whole batch bitter.
- **Fresh mint** bruised lightly and added to the jug.
- **Ginger**: a few thin slices of fresh ginger in a black or green tea cold-brew.
- **Berries or stone fruit**: a handful of sliced peaches or crushed berries steeped with the tea.
- **Citrus peel**: a strip of orange peel with a black tea brew.
- **Herbal and caffeine-free**: peppermint, rooibos, hibiscus and lemongrass all make good iced tea, and are handy for afternoons if you're cutting back on caffeine.

## Sweetening (if you want to)

Sugar doesn't dissolve well in cold liquid, so it sinks to the bottom. Two easy fixes:

- **Dissolve it while hot** if you're using the hot-brew method.
- **Make a simple syrup** by stirring equal parts sugar and hot water until clear, then cooling it. Keep it in a small bottle in the fridge and add a little to each glass.

Honey also dissolves more easily in warm tea than cold. Start with less than you think; it's easy to add more.

## Making it work at your desk

- **Use a lidded bottle or insulated tumbler.** It keeps the tea cold and stops spills near your keyboard.
- **Put a coaster down.** Iced drinks sweat, especially in humid weather.
- **Fill a bottle for the day in the morning,** rather than walking to the fridge every hour (unless the walk is the point, which isn't a bad idea either).
- **Keep caffeine in mind.** Black and green teas contain caffeine. If you drink a lot over the day, consider switching to herbal blends after lunch.

## The one-line version

Put tea and cold water in a jug before bed, strain it in the morning, pour it over ice. Everything else is fine-tuning.
