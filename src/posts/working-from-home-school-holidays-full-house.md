For a lot of people, the summer school holidays don't line up neatly with their own leave. Queensland state school summer holidays run from mid-December to late January, which can leave weeks of working from home in a house full of children, visiting relatives, a loud TV and someone who wants to know where the sunscreen is.

You can't make a full house as quiet as an empty one. But you can make it workable. These are the strategies that tend to help most, roughly in the order you'd set them up.

## Have the conversation before the holidays start

Most of the friction in a busy house comes from people not knowing what to expect. Before the holidays begin, sit down with the people you live with, including older kids, and agree on a few things:

- **Your working hours,** and which parts of the day are hardest to interrupt.
- **What counts as an emergency** (injury, something broken, someone at the door) and what can wait (snacks, screen-time negotiations, lost thongs).
- **Who's "on duty"** for the kids at which times, if there's more than one adult working.
- **What the house looks like during calls**: no vacuuming, no blasting music, and the dog let out beforehand.

Write the main points on a sheet on the fridge. It sounds overly formal, but it saves repeating the same negotiation every morning.

## Make a simple "do not disturb" signal

Children (and adults) can't always tell the difference between you checking email and you presenting to a client. A clear visual signal helps:

- a sign on the door: green side for "come in", red side for "only if it's urgent"
- headphones on meaning "on a call"
- a lamp or desk light that's on when you can't be interrupted

Use it honestly. If the red sign is up all day, people stop respecting it. Save it for the times that really matter.

## Split the day into focus and flexible blocks

Not every hour of work needs total quiet. Look at your work and sort it into two piles:

1. **Focus work**: calls, writing, anything with a deadline or that needs you to think hard.
2. **Flexible work**: email, admin, updates, reading, tasks you can do with interruptions.

Then schedule focus work into the quietest times of day. For many households, that's early morning before everyone's up, or a block after lunch when kids are winding down or out. Flexible work fills the noisier parts of the day.

If your job allows it, shifting your hours (starting early and finishing early, for example) can buy back a couple of quiet hours.

## Bunch your calls together

Every call needs a quiet room and a calm house. That's easier to arrange two or three times a day than ten times a day.

- Ask whether some meetings can be emails or a message thread instead.
- Group the ones that remain into one or two windows.
- Let the household know those windows in advance, so outings, screen time or quiet play can be planned around them.

## Protect your space (even if it's small)

If you have a separate room with a door, use it and close it. If you don't, try to claim a corner that's at least away from the main thoroughfare: not the kitchen bench, not the lounge room where the TV lives.

A few things help in shared spaces:

- **Noise-cancelling headphones,** or earplugs, for focus work.
- **A folding screen or bookshelf** to create a visual boundary.
- **A box or tub** for your work things, so they can be packed away when the table is needed for a jigsaw or lunch.

## Share the internet fairly

A house full of people streaming, gaming and video-calling can slow everyone's connection down.

- **Ask for big downloads and game updates to happen overnight,** or outside your call windows.
- **Use a cable** from your computer to the router if you can, which is usually more reliable than Wi-Fi.
- **Turn off your own video** in large meetings if the connection is struggling.
- **Check your router's settings.** Some let you prioritise a particular device, which can help on busy days.

## Plan the kids' days a bit, not a lot

You don't have to run a holiday program at home. But a little structure stops the "I'm bored" knocks on your door.

- **A loose daily rhythm** (outside time in the cooler morning, quiet activity after lunch, screen time during your call window) gives them something to expect.
- **A bored box**: craft supplies, books, puzzles, Lego, things they're allowed to use without asking.
- **Holiday programs or vacation care** for some days, if that suits your family and budget. Councils, libraries and community centres often run holiday activities, so check what's on locally.
- **Swapping days with another family** can work well. You take their kids one day; they take yours another.
- **Older kids can help,** with clear jobs and some reward for it.

## Be honest with your work

Most managers and colleagues have their own version of this problem in summer. It's usually fine to say, "I've got the kids home over the break, so I'm best reached between 7 and 12, and I'll be back online at 3." People generally respond better to a clear, honest pattern than to someone who's half-present all day.

If the load is genuinely unworkable, it's worth asking about flexible hours, a short period of leave, or reduced hours over the holidays. Those conversations are easier to have before you're exhausted.

## Lower the bar where you can

A full house over summer is not the time for your most ambitious project. Pick the work that truly has to happen, do it well, and let the rest move slower. Make sure you get some breaks yourself, preferably away from the desk and outside of the kids' requests.

And when the holidays end and the house goes quiet again, you'll be surprised how fast you get through your inbox.
