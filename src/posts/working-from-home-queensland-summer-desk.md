Most advice about setting up a home desk comes from places with mild weather. It's all about ergonomics and cable tidying, and nobody mentions that by 2 pm in January your west-facing spare room can feel like a greenhouse. If you work from home anywhere warm in Australia, the season changes how you should arrange the room, not just how you cool it.

I've shifted my own desk three times over the years, and these are the things I'd tell a friend before they carry the furniture anywhere.

## Start with the sun, not the power point

Before you decide where the desk goes, spend one afternoon watching where the sun lands. In the southern hemisphere the sun tracks across the northern sky, so rooms facing west pick up strong, low light late in the day, and north-facing windows get sun for most of the day in winter but are easier to shade in summer because the sun is higher.

What this means in practice:

- **West-facing windows** are usually the hottest in summer afternoons. If your desk has to be near one, plan to shade it.
- **East-facing windows** give you morning light, which is nice for a start-of-day desk but can be glary on a screen before about 10 am.
- **A desk facing a window** gives you pretty views but a lot of screen glare. A desk with the window to your side is often a better compromise.
- **A desk with a window behind you** puts reflections on your monitor.

Do a simple test. Open your laptop or monitor at the planned spot at the time of day you work most, and look at the screen. If you're squinting, move it.

## Shade the window, not just the room

Air-conditioning works much harder when the sun is pouring through glass. Cheap ways to cut the heat before it gets into the room:

- **Blockout or thermal-backed curtains**, drawn in the afternoon. They work best if they fall close to the window and sit tight against the wall at the sides.
- **Sunshade or reflective film** on the window. Check whether you're renting and whether you need permission, and look for removable options that don't leave marks.
- **External shade**, such as an awning or outdoor blind, which stops heat before it gets to the glass. This is generally the most effective but also the least renter-friendly option.
- **Plants outside the window**, which provide some dappled shade without darkening the room too much.

If you rent, curtains and a removable film are usually the easiest option to bring with you to the next place.

## Let air move

Air conditioners cool, but moving air makes you feel cooler. A small desk fan or a ceiling fan lets you set the air-con a bit higher while staying comfortable, and a fan can be enough on its own on milder days. A few small notes:

- Point a fan at yourself, not at your papers. Loose notes and receipts scatter easily.
- A fan with a weighty base will survive being nudged by a cat or a dog.
- If you use the air-con, keep the room door closed, or you're cooling the whole house.
- Don't have the cold air blowing directly on you for hours. If you find yourself getting a stiff neck or dry eyes, redirect the vents or move your chair.

## Pick a chair that won't make you sweat

An upholstered office chair is comfortable in winter and sticky in January. Mesh-backed chairs let air through. If you already have a padded chair you love, a breathable seat cover can help. Leather and vinyl are the warmest to sit on in summer.

If you work at a dining table, check that the chair isn't also a plastic outdoor chair you've brought inside. Plastic is cool at first and sweaty by lunchtime.

## Laptops get hot too

A laptop on your lap or on a soft surface blocks vents and runs warmer than it should. In a hot room, it'll often throttle itself and the fan will get noisy. Keep it on a hard, flat surface, or on a stand with air gaps underneath. A cheap folding stand lifts the screen to a better height and gives the vents room to breathe. Keep it out of direct sun, particularly if it's sitting near a window, and don't leave it in a hot car.

## Water within arm's reach

It sounds obvious, but when you're deep in a task it's easy to realise at 3 pm that you haven't had a drink all day. Keep a bottle or insulated tumbler on the desk, and fill it before you sit down. An insulated cup keeps water cold for longer in a warm room and, unlike a glass, doesn't leave rings on timber. Use a coaster under anything that sweats with condensation.

## Humidity and paper

If you live in a humid part of the country, paper curls, sticky notes drop off, and cardboard files go soft. Small fixes:

- Store important papers in a box or a drawer rather than on an open shelf.
- Use a dehumidifier or a small tub of moisture-absorbing crystals in a closed cupboard, if damp is a regular issue.
- Keep books away from external walls and window sills.
- Keep a spare clip for the pile of papers, because the fan will find them.

## Cables, power and storms

Summer storms are common in many parts of Australia and can cause power surges. A decent surge-protected power board is a cheap safeguard for a computer and monitor. If you have a lightning storm approaching and a deadline looming, save your work often. It's also worth knowing where your power board switch is, so you can turn everything off at the wall quickly.

If you're going to tidy your cables at the same time, use velcro ties rather than permanent zip ties so you can move things around later.

## Choose your hours around the heat

If you have flexibility, schedule the work that needs the most concentration for the cooler parts of the day, such as the morning, and the lighter tasks, like emails and admin, for the middle of the afternoon. When you can't, take a break and step outside for a few minutes, or sit somewhere cooler.

Also remember the room itself will be cooler in the morning, after the night's temperatures have dropped. Start early, take a longer lunch, and finish when the room is at its warmest if you can arrange it.

## A quick summer desk checklist

- Desk position checked for glare and afternoon sun
- Curtains or blinds drawn before the heat builds
- Fan or air-con set up, and door closed
- Laptop on a hard surface or stand
- Water bottle filled and in reach
- Surge-protected power board in use
- Papers stored away from damp
- A short break planned for the hottest part of the afternoon

## And a small bit of personality

Once the comfort basics are sorted, make the desk a nice place to be. A small plant that's happy with a bit of indoor light, a good lamp for stormy afternoons, and one or two things you like looking at is plenty. The most pleasant home desks I've seen aren't the ones with the most gear. They're the ones where someone has thought about the light, the air and where the cup goes.

If you only change one thing this weekend, spend ten minutes at your desk in the middle of the afternoon and notice what's annoying you. Fix that first.
